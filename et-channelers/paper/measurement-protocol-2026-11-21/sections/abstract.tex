Studies of online audio increasingly rest on automatic transcripts and language-model labels; for long recordings with silence and several voices, neither has a human reference. We audit a Whisper-family pipeline that transcribed \NTranscribedRecordings{} recordings, from the newest \NPerCreator{} uploads of \NCreators{} creators chosen because they describe their practice as channelling (a judgment sample), in fixed 30-s windows. No word error rate is reported, and machine comparisons are read as agreement. A re-decode differs from platform captions on \AnchorCaptionMeanPct\% of caption words (creator mean). A 15-s grid shift changes \GsChangedPct\% of words, edits near seams being about \GsNearSeamRatio{} times the uniform share, and a placebo grid removes the onset spike at window starts. A window-end courtesy phrase that appears to be inserted occurs \ThanksPerSpeechHour{} times per speech hour on one grid position; segments cover the last, partial window least; pace has a creator intraclass correlation of \IccPace{}, so the creator is the unit. We also document an internally registered (hashes and commits), then amended, protocol for model-coded labels: a hold-out of whole speakers and ten hypotheses on claimed voices, asks to buy or enrol, forecasts and shared vocabulary. Projections made before any hold-out label announced H1, H4 and H6 as probably not determinable. \ResAbstract{} None passed the Holm step. H3, tested earlier under a registered rule not calibrated at the creator level, was withdrawn by the project owner. Instruments gated during design did not all pass on a hold-out reliability sample; no person checked any label. No measure in this study can tell whether a non-human source speaks; every measure describes the speech in these recordings and how the creators present it. We report no finding about channelling.
