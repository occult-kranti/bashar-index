    Methods

    How candidates were found
    Rules over every sentence of the stored texts: a future marker (will, going to, coming), a prediction word (predict, prophecy, forecast, warn) or a prophecy theme (shift, ascension, disclosure, first contact, collapse and the like), together with a time expression. Each time expression becomes a target window counted from the date said: “in two years” from a 2021 text is 2023, “by 2030” keeps one bound only, “soon” gets no bounds and is not on this page. Only candidates with a target window appear here.
    Date said and its basis
    The published date of the item where the catalogue has one; else a full date in the title; else a year and month in the address of the page; else unknown. The precision shown is the precision of that source.
    Copies
    Near-copies of a statement (shared five-word runs, Jaccard at least one half) are collapsed; the first occurrence is shown.
    What the blinded sample measured
    A fresh sample of 99 windowed candidates, labelled blind by two automated (agent) coders who did not see the rules (agreement kappa 0.96), put real predictions at about 56% (weighted by person; 95% interval about 47% to 65%), from about a fifth to nine in ten by person. The rest are misparsed dates, quoted past years or rhetorical futures. No accuracy is scored on this page.
    Reading the timeline
    One row per group, one mark per statement at the first year of its target window, a bar to the last year. Marks that would overlap are stacked into a pill with a count; zooming in spreads them. The shaded area is the past and the dashed line is the build date. Hovering or focusing a mark draws a dotted lead from the year the statement was said to its target. The mini-map below the chart shows the density of all target years and the window in view. A probe year (the Expected for filter, or the y key in the chart) draws a solid line across the rows and counts the windows that cover it; the lead lines switch draws a hairline from the year said to the target for every statement in view. Every value in the chart is also in the table view.
    Sharing a view
    The view lives in the address after the hash sign: the filters (p, k, f, s, d, e, b, a for years ahead, y for the probe year, q), the window (x) and whether the list follows it (w), the lead lines (l), the grouping (g), the view (v), the sort (o), a pinned statement (i) and full screen (fs, the expanded mode when opened from a link). A link's pinned statement refers to this build's numbering.
    What is withheld and why
    No transcript and no audio: a quote of at most 30 words per statement (words are whitespace tokens with a letter or digit in them; the closing ellipsis is not a word), at most 5% of an item's words across the page (for speech-to-text, 5% of the transcript's words) and at most 3,000 quoted words per person; where the share leaves no room, the time expression stands in. No questioner is named: a sentence that opens as someone else's turn, a bare "Questioner" opener included, or that names a person on a private list kept by the owner, shows no quote. No private location: items are named by id and linked to their public page where one is known; a podcast episode known only by its audio file has no link.
    The guard
    Before the page is written, a guard recomputes the quote lengths and shares, checks every link, looks for speaker prefixes, addresses and private locations, and compares the whole page against the private texts for any run of more than thirty words anywhere and any five-word run outside the quotes. If any check fails, nothing is written.
